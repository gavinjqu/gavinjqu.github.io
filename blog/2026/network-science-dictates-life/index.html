The Network Science That Dictates Your Life

Created on January 25, 2026

2026   ·   network-science   labor-economics   economics   ·   social-science

A few days ago, I was on a walk with a friend, and our conversation drifted into a familiar paradox of how the world can feel so vast yet tiny at the same time. Naturally, I followed up by asking him the age-old question: Would you rather be a big fish in a small pond or a small fish in a big pond? They chose “big fish in a small pond” because of the confidence and visibility one can receive. Truth is, the final choice is probably a function of a high-dimensional set of preferences and unobservables on top of timing. Out of the two choices, each offers distinct advantages and drawbacks, many of which show up repeatedly in economics research on mobility, inequality, and network science. Economic networks can be both mindblowing and terrifying at the same time once you understand the implications.

The Phoenix and the Chicken

Growing up in a Chinese household, you’ll often hear the following proverb: “Better be the head of a chicken than the tail of a phoenix.” Sentiments I usually disagree with, but it does demonstrate the clear tradeoff between relative standing and absolute ability. Essentially, it implies that one has more influence and autonomy being the “head of the chicken”, while being the “tail of the phoenix” puts you in the company of exceptional peers, exciting but competitive, and definitely less visibility to start off with.

It’s Who You Know

There is a famous paradox in the social sciences called the friendship paradox, which describes how majority of the time, your friends will have more friends than the number of friends you have. This is measured by Degree Centrality, calculated by summing up the number of edges (direct connections) that a node (an individual) is connected to. If you think about it, it makes sense that you’re more likely to be friends with someone who has a lot of friends than someone that don’t.

The same concept can also be used to illustrate how dense networks spread ideas and contagion faster than sparse ones. Malcolm Gladwell coined the terms “connectors” and “mavens” to describe a simliar phenemenon that resulted in the Covid-19 pandemic and social media influencers. Many of us use social networks that facilitate the spread of ideas and funny cat memes; in fact, as you’re reading this, our lives have become more intertwined in this network of ideas.

In Matt Jackson’s book The Human Networks, he goes into detail describing the ingenious implications of networks using evidence from the Medici, Facebook friends, and Google’s Page Rank.

Similars Attract

As job seekers and new grads in the post-2023 labor market, many of us have this idiomatic approach to hunting for that next role: “cold email and get referrals”. In fact, in almost all professions, a majority of jobs are found through referrals. In other words, you have to know someone who can get your resume through the “gate of Mordor” and the black pit of coverletter despair to get an interview.

The stronger link version of the above phenomenon is explained by connection via close friends, and the weaker link version of it comes from acquaintance-to-acquaintance connections.

What is really fascinating to me is the concept of Homophily, a word with Greek roots describing how people with similar culture, education, and beliefs are found in clusters. Creating inequality that is perpetuated by the small quantity of bias in preference, this can be seen in neighborhoods, hierarchical social structures such as India’s caste system, and the labor market. Homophily creates a system where between-group differences are exacerbated by an individual’s preference to self-select into different clusters based on homogeneous demographics, which in turn further divides the differences between groups, creating this endogenous loop in our model.

Take a moment to look around your network on LinkedIn, Discord groups, or Facebook friends — we are the environment we live in.

The “Wisdom” of the Crowd

The next concept extends homophily to the game theory of coordination, the famous restaurant example you all have been waiting for, no probability theory required for this one, but some basic game theory knowledge is assumed.

Imagine you’re wandering in an unfamiliar neighborhood around dinner time and looking for a restaurant. There are two choices in front of you, the first one is filled with empty tables, while the second one has a few patrons already. Which one are you more likely to choose, given no other information (Google reviews, etc.)? The optimal choice here for most rational individuals would be to walk into the one with more people.

Now, in the second scenario, imagine both restaurants are empty when you show up, and you still have the same set of choices with all the same preferences as before, which one would you choose now? All else being equal and without any prior knowledge, your choice here is probably as good as random. You’re now in the shoes of those other people in scenario one who probably made the choice by random chance. However, being the first in this set of players (participants) in this coordination game have implication that affect every other player who shows up after you. Cognitively, it is less costly for subsequent players to make choices similar to those of earlier participants than to spend 10 minutes reading Google reviews or employing an algorithmic approach.

Information externality describes the phenomenon where an individual’s disclosure of information has unintended consequences for other non-participants. A player’s well-being is determined not only by their own choices but also by the choices of their neighbors, coworkers, and friends.

To sum it up, choose your friends and networks wisely, because your well-being and future career depend on those choices.
